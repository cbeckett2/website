Research

I am a medical sociologist at the University of Chicago. I study how families sustain dementia care, and how the practical knowledge that care produces comes to be recognized or disregarded.

The work is interpretive in method and comparative in design. It begins with long-form interviews in Italy and the United States and extends to national panel surveys of aging. Two arguments organize it: that burden and capability are distinct dimensions of caregiving, not opposite ends of one continuum; and that authority over care is assembled from audiences whose recognition does not substitute for one another. This work appears in the Journal of Health and Social Behavior and Sociology of Health & Illness (forthcoming). A separate study, on migrants' encounters with the bureaucracy of Italy's health system, appears in Current Sociology.

Projects

Caregiver flourishing: burden and capability as distinct dimensions

Research on family caregiving has been organized around burden for four decades. The burden tradition, from the measures of Zarit, Reever, and Bach-Peterson (1980) to Pearlin's stress-process model (1989), indexes what care extracts from caregivers; it offers no comparable account of what they construct. Drawing on 20 in-depth interviews and twelve months of fieldwork in Emilia-Romagna, Italy, and the rural Midwestern United States, I developed the caregiver flourishing framework, published in the Journal of Health and Social Behavior in 2026.

Its central argument is that burden and capability vary as distinct dimensions, as Keyes (2002) showed for mental illness and mental health. Caregivers reported profound strain while exercising expertise that had sustained care for years, so the absence of burden does not indicate flourishing and its presence does not preclude it. The dimensions are not sealed from one another: strain was often the substrate from which capability was built.

Flourishing is defined as sustainable capacity under conditions: the capacity to continue care while preserving connection, mastery, identity, and meaning. The four domains are co-constitutive, each supplying conditions the others require. The definition rests on Nussbaum's (2011) distinction between internal capabilities, the competencies a person has developed, and combined capabilities, which exist only where external conditions allow those competencies to be exercised. A caregiver can hold the first without the second. Capacity in this account is therefore constitutively relational: it emerges when structural arrangements and cultural logics, among them predictable respite, service architecture, kinship expectations, and clinical recognition, allow what a caregiver knows to function.

A companion study with Elisa Castellaccio, forthcoming in Sociology of Health & Illness, asks what happens when someone must perform intimate touch for which their gendered body holds no repertoire. Drawing on two ethnographic streams with eight men caring for female kin with dementia in Emilia-Romagna, it finds that the contradiction between kinship obligation and masculine socialization resolves in two ways: corporeal delegation, which routes intimate labor onto the racialized reproductive labor of migrant women, or corporeal reorganization, in which men acquire new capacities through repeated, scaffolded practice. Where reorganization occurred, the change in relational meaning followed the acquisition of capacity; it did not precede it. The article extends Twigg's account of body work by identifying an inequality prior to the question of who performs intimate care.

Next: care arrangements and caregiver health

The framework leaves a question that interviews cannot settle: whether supportive care arrangements build capability or mark caregivers who were already equipped. My next project tests this prospectively in the National Study of Caregiving, a national panel of family caregivers linked to the National Health and Aging Trends Study. I will test whether three features of a care arrangement predict capability, activity restriction, and depressive symptoms one year later, net of earlier levels and care demands. The features are dependable help (another person takes defined tasks on a regular schedule), recognition (co-caregivers know and respect what the caregiver does), and family disagreement. The same models test the dimensional claim directly: whether capability reduces later depressive symptoms or coexists with them.

In exploratory analyses of two cohorts of the National Study of Caregiving, family disagreement was associated with greater activity restriction. A complementary analysis of the National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project tests whether participation in community activities predicts five-year change in depressive symptoms among older adults who are caregiving at baseline.

Paired interviews, conducted separately with primary caregivers and co-caregivers in rural Midwestern families and repeated after twelve months, will explain how these features operate as care demands change. The integrated findings will specify a family care-planning session, co-designed with caregivers and rural program staff, that addresses task sharing, the communication of caregivers' practical knowledge, and the resolution of disagreement.

This program is proposed in a K99/R00 application to the National Institute on Aging, currently under review (fingers crossed).

Lay expertise and recognition: whose judgment counts

Doing difficult work well does not make one an authorized expert. A credential carries authorization ahead of its holder, from one encounter to the next. Where competence develops without one, as it does in family dementia care, standing must be secured from each audience directly. Legitimacy Portfolios, a working paper, theorizes how that happens. It carries Abbott's account of professional jurisdiction to uncredentialed practice and joins it to Glaeser's analysis of validation through encounter.

The central claim is that audiences confer categorically different standings. Clinicians recognize competence: knowledge of the condition and its trajectory. Families authorize warrant: the standing to be the person who provides or directs the care. Peers confer membership: recognition as someone living the same situation. These standings do not convert into one another, which is what separates a portfolio from symbolic capital. Competence is not warrant, and abundant recognition from one audience cannot make good its absence from another.

Three conditions govern whether a portfolio consolidates: non-substitutability across audiences, each audience's accumulated knowledge of the particular caregiver, and normative alignment between the recognition offered and the caregiver's own account of what the care requires. Infrastructure decides who does the assembling. Where clinical, family, and peer settings are linked, caregivers move from one domain to the next; where they are episodic and segmented, coordination across them becomes the caregiver's own labor.

The third condition makes recognition two-sided. Those who receive recognition also evaluate it: they accept, qualify, or refuse what an audience extends, according to their own account of what the care requires. The question I most want to answer follows from this: how people decide, across encounters, whose judgment to treat as authoritative and whose to disregard. How those accounts form, in biography, social position, and a particular relation of dependency, is what the project takes up next. The problem reaches any setting where competence must be authorized without a credential, including community health work, peer support in recovery, and patient advocacy.

Migration and access to health care

A separate line of research asks how a formally inclusive health system produces unequal access. A study published in Current Sociology in 2025 draws on the narratives of Nigerian migrants in Emilia-Romagna to show how legal status, administrative requirements, and interpersonal ties structure routes to care, and how distrust and bureaucratic barriers leave access dependent on the relationships people can mobilize. It shares with the caregiving work an attention to how laypeople navigate health institutions.
